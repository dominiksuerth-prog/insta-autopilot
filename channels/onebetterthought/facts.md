## Habits
- Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010), European Journal of Social Psychology: 96 adults each picked one daily eating, drinking or activity habit. For participants whose data could be modelled, the median time until the behaviour felt automatic was 66 days; individual estimates ranged from 18 to 254 days. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. Simpler behaviours became automatic faster than harder ones (e.g. exercise). The "21 days" idea traces back to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics, an observation, not an experiment.
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology: meta-analysis of 94 studies on implementation intentions ("If situation X, then I do Y"). If-then plans had a medium-to-large positive effect on reaching goals (d = 0.65).

## Mind traps
- Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky (2000), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: students wore an embarrassing Barry Manilow T-shirt into a room of peers. Wearers estimated about 50% of observers noticed; only about 25% actually could name who was on the shirt. In a follow-up with shirts people liked, wearers again guessed about 50%, fewer than 10% noticed. Name: spotlight effect.

## Emotions
- Lieberman et al. (2007), Psychological Science: 30 participants viewed emotional faces in an fMRI scanner. Labeling the emotion with a word (e.g. "angry") reduced the response of the amygdala, the brain's alarm system, and increased activity in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex. Name: affect labeling ("name it to tame it").

## Focus & energy
- Oppezzo & Schwartz (2014), Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition: walking (indoors on a treadmill or outside) increased creative idea generation compared with sitting; on average creative output rose by about 60%.

## Focus & phones
- Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017), Journal of the Association for Consumer Research: two experiments with nearly 800 smartphone users. Participants silenced their phones and placed them either on the desk, in a pocket/bag, or in another room, then took tests of working memory capacity and fluid intelligence. The more noticeable the phone, the lower the available cognitive capacity – people with the phone in another room did best, even though they did not use or check it. Costs were highest for people most dependent on their phone. Name: "brain drain". Honest limit: later replication attempts found smaller or no effects – say "in this study".

## Motivation & fresh starts
- Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014), Management Science: three field studies. Google searches for "diet", gym visits and sign-ups for goal commitments all increased after "temporal landmarks" – the start of a new week, month, year or semester, a birthday or a holiday. Explanation: landmarks feel like a new chapter that separates us from past failures. Name: fresh start effect.

## Self-talk & setbacks
- Breines & Chen (2012), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin: four experiments with university students. In one, students failed a difficult test; those who received a self-compassionate message ("if you had difficulty, you're not alone, don't be too hard on yourself") afterwards spent more time studying for a second test than those who got a self-esteem boost or nothing. In other experiments self-compassion increased the motivation to make amends after a mistake and to change a personal weakness.

## Stress
- Jamieson, Mendes, Blackstock & Schmader (2010), Journal of Experimental Social Psychology: students preparing for the GRE took a practice test. Half were told that feeling aroused/nervous before a test can actually improve performance (reappraisal); the others got no such information. The reappraisal group scored higher on the math section – and again on the real GRE one to three months later. Name: stress reappraisal.

## Gratitude
- Emmons & McCullough (2003), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology: three studies. In Study 1, students wrote down five things they were grateful for once a week (for about ten weeks); comparison groups listed hassles or neutral events. The gratitude group felt better about their lives as a whole, was more optimistic about the coming week, reported fewer physical symptoms and exercised more regularly. In a daily version, the gratitude group more often reported helping someone or offering emotional support.
